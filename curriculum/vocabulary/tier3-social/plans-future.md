## Common Collocations
- **tener ganas de + infinitive** — to feel like doing something; casual expression of desire
- **pensar + infinitive** — to plan to do something; common in everyday speech
- **cumplir una meta / un objetivo** — to achieve a goal
- **hacer planes** — to make plans
- **cambiar de planes** — to change plans; note **de**
- **a largo/corto plazo** — in the long/short term
- **salir bien/mal** — to turn out well/badly; "Espero que salga bien"
- **tomar una decisión** — to make a decision; uses **tomar**, not **hacer**

## False Cognates and Tricky Translations
- **el éxito** vs **exit**: **Éxito** means success. Exit is **la salida**. "Tener éxito" = to be successful.
- **realizar** vs **realize**: **Realizar** means to carry out / accomplish. To realize (become aware) is **darse cuenta de**. "Realicé mi sueño" = "I accomplished my dream," NOT "I realized my dream" (in the awareness sense).
- **actual** vs **actual**: **Actual** means current/present. Actual (real) is **real** or **verdadero**. **Actualmente** = currently, not actually.
- **pretender** vs **pretend**: **Pretender** means to intend or try. "Pretendo terminar hoy" = "I intend to finish today." To pretend is **fingir**.

## Dialect Variations
- **to postpone**: **postergar** (Latin America) vs **aplazar** (Spain). Both understood everywhere.
- **deadline**: **el plazo** is universal. **La fecha límite** is also common and transparent.
- **to feel like**: **tener ganas de** is universal. Mexico also uses **se me antoja** for cravings/desires (especially food-related).
- **ojalá**: universal and high-frequency. From Arabic "inshallah" — reflects the Moorish linguistic legacy in Spanish.

## Cultural and Pragmatic Notes
- **Ojalá and the subjunctive**: **Ojalá** is one of the most-used words that always triggers subjunctive. With present subjunctive ("Ojalá venga"), it expresses a possible hope. With past subjunctive ("Ojalá viniera"), it expresses an unlikely or impossible wish. This distinction is subtle and powerful.
- **Plans vs. commitments**: In many Latin American cultures, stating a plan ("vamos a vernos") can be more aspirational than in US culture. Context and tone determine how firm the commitment is. This is cultural, not dishonesty.
- **"Esperar" double duty**: **Esperar** means both "to hope" and "to wait." "Espero que llegue" could mean "I hope he arrives" or "I'm waiting for him to arrive." Context disambiguates.
- **Conditional for politeness**: The conditional is not just for hypotheticals. "Me gustaría ir" (I would like to go) is more polite than "Quiero ir" (I want to go). This applies to all request-making in formal or unfamiliar contexts.

## Pronunciation Alerts
- **ojalá** — stress on the last syllable: o-ja-LÁ. The accent is mandatory.
- **propósito** — esdrújula: pro-PÓ-si-to. English speakers tend to stress the third syllable.
- **objetivo** — stress on the third syllable: ob-je-TI-vo.
- **éxito** — esdrújula: É-xi-to. NOT ex-I-to. The accent on the first syllable is critical.
- **plazo** — two syllables: PLA-so. The **z** is pronounced as /s/ in Latin America.
