## Common Collocations
- **buscar trabajo** — to look for work; standard phrase (not "buscar un empleo" in casual speech)
- **tener una reunión** — to have a meeting
- **pedir un aumento** — to ask for a raise
- **trabajar de** + profession — to work as: "Trabajo de mesero" (I work as a waiter)
- **trabajar en** + place/field — to work at/in: "Trabajo en un banco"
- **estar desempleado** — to be unemployed; uses **estar** (current state)
- **ganarse la vida** — to make a living; reflexive in this expression
- **mandar un correo** — to send an email; shortened from correo electrónico
- **cumplir con el plazo** — to meet the deadline
- **dar de baja** — to terminate/cancel (employment or service)

## False Cognates and Tricky Translations
- **la carrera** vs **career**: Partially overlapping — **carrera** means both "career" and "university degree." "Estudio la carrera de medicina" = "I'm studying to get a degree in medicine," not "I'm studying medicine as a career."
- **la firma** vs **firm**: **Firma** primarily means "signature." It can mean "firm/company" but this usage is less common. To sign = **firmar**.
- **asistir** vs **assist**: **Asistir** means "to attend" (Asistí a la reunión). To assist = **ayudar**.
- **el éxito** vs **exit**: **Éxito** means "success." An exit is **la salida**.
- **la agenda** vs **agenda**: **Agenda** in Spanish usually means "planner/day planner" (the physical object). The meeting agenda = **el orden del día**.
- **despedir** vs **despedirse**: **Despedir** = to fire someone. **Despedirse** (reflexive) = to say goodbye. Very different meanings depending on reflexive pronoun.

## Dialect Variations
- **computer**: **la computadora** (Latin America), **el ordenador** (Spain). Note gender change.
- **cell phone**: **el celular** (Latin America), **el móvil** (Spain)
- **resume**: **el currículum** (most regions), **la hoja de vida** (Colombia)
- **training**: **la capacitación** (Latin America), **la formación** (Spain)
- **internship**: **la pasantía** (Latin America), **las prácticas** (Spain)
- **boss (informal)**: **el patrón / la patrona** (Mexico, can sound old-fashioned), **el jefe / la jefa** (universal)
- **to work (informal/slang)**: **chambear** (Mexico, informal), **currar** (Spain, informal), **laburar** (Argentina, informal)

## Cultural and Pragmatic Notes
- **Work conversations as social currency**: Asking "¿A qué te dedicas?" (What do you do?) is a standard introductory question across Latin America and Spain. It is not considered intrusive.
- **Salary talk**: Discussing specific salaries is less taboo in many Latin American cultures than in the US, but context matters. Among friends, "¿Cuánto ganas?" is not unusual.
- **Formality at work**: **Usted** is standard between employees and managers in most of Latin America, especially in initial interactions. Mexico tends to shift to **tú** faster in casual workplaces. Colombia often maintains **usted** even among close colleagues.
- **Preterite for work history**: Narrating past jobs uses preterite for bounded events: "Trabajé en esa empresa por tres años" (I worked there for three years — completed). Use imperfect for descriptions: "Era un buen ambiente" (It was a good environment — background).
- **Future plans with ir + a**: Career aspirations naturally use this structure: "Voy a buscar un nuevo trabajo" (I'm going to look for a new job).

## Pronunciation Alerts
- **fábrica** — esdrújula: FÁ-bri-ca. English speakers tend to say fa-BRI-ca.
- **reunión** — stress on final syllable: re-u-NIÓN. Four syllables, not three.
- **teléfono** — esdrújula: te-LÉ-fo-no. English stress pattern differs.
- **correo** — two syllables for the **rr**: co-RRE-o. The double r is a trill.
- **computadora** — stress on the penultimate: com-pu-ta-DO-ra. Do not stress like English "computer."
- **jefe** — the **j** is Spanish /x/ (guttural): HE-fe, not "Jeff."
