## Common Collocations
- **tener hambre** — to be hungry (literally "to have hunger"); NOT "ser/estar hambriento"
- **tener sed** — to be thirsty (literally "to have thirst")
- **pedir la cuenta** — to ask for the check
- **dejar propina** — to leave a tip
- **de beber** — to drink (¿Qué quiere de beber?)
- **a la plancha** — grilled (on a flat grill)
- **al horno** — baked / oven-roasted
- **bien cocido** — well done (meat)
- **término medio** — medium (meat, in Mexico); **al punto** in Spain
- **me trae** — could you bring me (polite request at restaurants)
- **¡buen provecho!** — enjoy your meal (said to others who are eating; equivalent of "bon appétit")

## False Cognates and Tricky Translations
- **la receta** vs "receipt": **Receta** means "recipe" (or medical prescription). A receipt is **el recibo**.
- **caliente** vs "hot" (spicy): **Caliente** means hot in temperature. Spicy hot is **picante**. English speakers often say "caliente" when they mean the food is spicy.
- **la entrada** vs "entrance": In a restaurant, **entrada** means appetizer/starter. The entrance (door) is also **la entrada**, but context distinguishes them.
- **el vaso** vs **la copa** vs **la taza**: **Vaso** = everyday drinking glass. **Copa** = stemmed glass (wine, cocktails). **Taza** = cup with handle (coffee, tea).

## Dialect Variations
- **mesero / camarero / mozo**: Mexico uses **mesero**. Spain uses **camarero**. Argentina/Uruguay use **mozo**. All mean "waiter."
- **jugo / zumo**: Latin America says **jugo** (juice). Spain says **zumo**. Both are universally understood.
- **frijoles / judías / porotos / habichuelas / caraotas**: Beans have a different name in virtually every country. Mexico: **frijoles**. Spain: **judías**. Argentina: **porotos**. Puerto Rico: **habichuelas**. Venezuela: **caraotas**.
- **plátano / banana / banano / guineo**: Mexico: **plátano** (for both plantain and banana). Central America: **banano** (banana) vs **plátano** (plantain). Spain: **plátano**. Caribbean: **guineo** (banana).
- **la comida**: In Mexico, this specifically means the big midday meal (roughly 2-4 PM). In other countries it may simply mean "food" or "meal."

## Cultural and Pragmatic Notes
- **Meal times**: In Mexico, **la comida** (the main meal) is typically 2-4 PM and is the largest meal of the day. **La cena** (dinner) is lighter, often 8-10 PM. In Spain, lunch is 2-3 PM and dinner is 9-10 PM or later. Eating dinner at 6 PM will mark you as a tourist.
- **Tipping**: Mexico: 10-15% is standard. Spain: tipping is not expected but rounding up or leaving small change is appreciated. Argentina: 10% is customary. Colombia: a service charge (propina sugerida) is often included and your server will ask if you want to include it.
- **¡Buen provecho!**: You say this to anyone who is eating — strangers included. If you walk past someone eating lunch in a park, it is polite to say **¡buen provecho!** It is the social norm, not an intrusion.
- **Getting the check**: Servers will not bring the check until you ask for it. Hovering or rushing diners out is considered rude. Wave the server over and say **La cuenta, por favor**.
- **Ordering with gustar**: English speakers instinctively say "Yo gusto el pollo." The correct structure is **Me gusta el pollo** (the chicken pleases me). The food is the grammatical subject.

## Pronunciation Alerts
- **cuchillo** — the **ll** sounds like English "y" in most of Latin America (cu-CHI-yo). In Argentina/Uruguay, like "sh" (cu-CHI-sho).
- **cerveza** — stress on the second syllable: cer-VE-sa. The **z** is pronounced "s" in Latin America, "th" in most of Spain.
- **quisiera** — stress on the second syllable: qui-SIE-ra. The **qu** is always "k" sound, never "kw."
- **jugo** — the **j** is a strong "h" sound (HU-go), not the English "j" as in "juice."
- **pollo** — two syllables: PO-yo (Latin America) or PO-lyo (parts of Spain). Never "pah-lo."
- **huevos** — the **h** is silent and the **ue** is a diphthong: WEH-vos, not "hoo-eh-vos."
