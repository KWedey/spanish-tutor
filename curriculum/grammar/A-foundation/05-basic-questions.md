# Basic Questions, Negation, and Double Negatives

## Overview
Questions and negation are fundamental to conversation — without them, a learner can only make statements. This concept covers question formation, the full set of question words, and Spanish negation, including the double negative construction that is grammatically required in Spanish but stigmatized in English. Mastering these structures unlocks the ability to ask for information, clarify meaning, and express what you do not have, know, or want.

## When to Teach
- Phase: A
- Prerequisites: A-01-present-regular
- L1 interference to preempt: negative-double-negative (severity: medium)

## The Pattern

### 1. Question Words (Interrogativos)

All question words carry a written accent mark when used in questions — always.

| Question word | Meaning | Example |
|--------------|---------|---------|
| **qué** | what | ¿**Qué** estudias? (What do you study?) |
| **quién / quiénes** | who (sg. / pl.) | ¿**Quién** es tu profesor? (Who is your teacher?) |
| **dónde** | where | ¿**Dónde** vives? (Where do you live?) |
| **cuándo** | when | ¿**Cuándo** llegas? (When do you arrive?) |
| **cómo** | how | ¿**Cómo** estás? (How are you?) |
| **por qué** | why | ¿**Por qué** estudias español? (Why do you study Spanish?) |
| **cuánto/a/os/as** | how much / how many | ¿**Cuántos** hermanos tienes? (How many siblings do you have?) |
| **cuál / cuáles** | which / which ones | ¿**Cuál** prefieres? (Which do you prefer?) |

**Cuál vs qué — a key distinction:**

| Use **qué** for | Use **cuál** for |
|-----------------|------------------|
| Definitions: ¿**Qué** es esto? (What is this?) | Selection: ¿**Cuál** es tu nombre? (What is your name? — which one from all names) |
| Categories: ¿**Qué** tipo de música te gusta? (What type of music do you like?) | Preferences: ¿**Cuál** prefieres, el rojo o el azul? (Which do you prefer, the red or the blue?) |
| Before a noun: ¿**Qué** libro lees? (What book are you reading?) | Before de: ¿**Cuál** de estos quieres? (Which of these do you want?) |
| General "what": ¿**Qué** haces? (What do you do?) | Specific "what/which": ¿**Cuál** es tu dirección? (What is your address?) |

> **Key rule:** Qué asks "what kind of thing?" — it requests a definition or category. Cuál asks "which one?" — it requests a selection from existing options. Before a noun, always use qué: "¿Qué libro?" never *"¿Cuál libro?"

### 2. Question Formation

Spanish has three ways to form questions, all requiring inverted punctuation marks ¿...? at the beginning and end.

**Inversion (verb before subject):**
- ¿**Hablas** español? (Do you speak Spanish?)
- ¿**Trabaja** María aquí? (Does María work here?)
- ¿**Estudian** tus hijos en esta escuela? (Do your kids study at this school?)

**Intonation only (same word order as a statement, voice rises):**
- ¿**Tú hablas** español? (You speak Spanish?)
- ¿**María trabaja** aquí? (María works here?)

**With a question word (question word first, then verb):**
- ¿**Dónde** vives? (Where do you live?)
- ¿**Cuántos** años tienes? (How old are you? — literally: How many years do you have?)
- ¿**Por qué** no comes? (Why aren't you eating?)

> **No "do" helper:** English uses "do/does" to form questions: "Do you speak Spanish?" Spanish never uses this — the verb alone carries the question: "¿Hablas español?" There is no equivalent of "do."

### 3. Negation and Double Negatives

**Basic negation:** Place **no** directly before the conjugated verb.

| Affirmative | Negative |
|-------------|----------|
| Hablo español. | **No** hablo español. |
| Tengo tiempo. | **No** tengo tiempo. |
| Ella vive aquí. | Ella **no** vive aquí. |

**Negative words:**

| Negative word | Meaning | Affirmative counterpart |
|--------------|---------|------------------------|
| **nada** | nothing | algo (something) |
| **nadie** | nobody | alguien (someone) |
| **nunca** | never | siempre (always) |
| **ninguno/a** | none, not any | alguno/a (some) |
| **tampoco** | neither, not either | también (also) |
| **ni...ni** | neither...nor | o...o (either...or) |

**The double negative rule:** When a negative word comes AFTER the verb, **no** is REQUIRED before the verb. This creates a double negative — and it is mandatory, not optional.

| Double negative (required) | Translation |
|---------------------------|-------------|
| **No** tengo **nada**. | I have nothing. / I don't have anything. |
| **No** conozco **a nadie** aquí. | I don't know anyone here. |
| **No** como carne **nunca**. | I never eat meat. |
| **No** tengo **ningún** problema. | I don't have any problem. |
| **No** quiero ir **tampoco**. | I don't want to go either. |
| **No** como **ni** carne **ni** pescado. | I eat neither meat nor fish. |

**Alternative word order:** When the negative word comes BEFORE the verb, no second **no** is needed.

| Negative word first (no double negative) | Same meaning |
|-----------------------------------------|--------------|
| **Nada** tengo. | I have nothing. |
| **Nadie** vive aquí. | Nobody lives here. |
| **Nunca** como carne. | I never eat meat. |
| **Tampoco** quiero ir. | I don't want to go either. |

> **Key rule:** Both word orders are correct. "No tengo nada" and "Nada tengo" mean the same thing. The first is more common in conversation. The second is slightly more emphatic or literary.

## Examples in Context

1. — ¿**Dónde** trabajas? — Trabajo **en** una oficina **en** el centro. (Where do you work? — I work in an office downtown.)
2. — ¿**Cuántos** hermanos tienes? — **No** tengo **ninguno**. (How many siblings do you have? — I don't have any.)
3. — ¿**Por qué** estudias español? — Porque quiero viajar a México. (Why do you study Spanish? — Because I want to travel to Mexico.)
4. — ¿**Quién** cocina en tu casa? — **Nadie** cocina bien. Comemos fuera. (Who cooks in your house? — Nobody cooks well. We eat out.)
5. — ¿**Cuál** es tu restaurante favorito? — **No** tengo **ninguno** favorito. (What is your favorite restaurant? — I don't have a favorite.)
6. — ¿**Cómo** vas al trabajo? — **Nunca** manejo. Siempre camino. (How do you get to work? — I never drive. I always walk.)
7. — ¿**Qué** haces los fines de semana? — **No** hago **nada** especial. (What do you do on weekends? — I don't do anything special.)
8. — ¿Hablas francés? — No, y **no** hablo italiano **tampoco**. (Do you speak French? — No, and I don't speak Italian either.)
9. — ¿**Cuándo** llegas a casa? — **No** llego **nunca** antes de las siete. (When do you get home? — I never arrive before seven.)
10. — ¿**Qué** tipo de música te gusta? — **No** me gusta **ni** el rock **ni** el pop. Prefiero la salsa. (What type of music do you like? — I like neither rock nor pop. I prefer salsa.)

## L1 Interference

**English habit:** English teaches that double negatives are wrong. "I don't have nothing" is considered uneducated in English — the "correct" form is "I don't have anything." This deeply ingrained rule causes English speakers to actively avoid double negatives in Spanish, producing errors.

**The core conflict:** The learner's English grammar instinct will fight the correct Spanish structure. When they want to say "I don't have anything," their brain will produce "No tengo algo" (mapping "anything" to algo) instead of the correct "No tengo nada." The learner must override years of English conditioning.

**Contrast pairs:**

| English instinct | Correct Spanish | Why |
|------------------|-----------------|-----|
| I don't have anything. (→ *No tengo algo.) | **No tengo nada.** | After no + verb, use nada (not algo) |
| I don't know anyone. (→ *No conozco a alguien.) | **No conozco a nadie.** | After no + verb, use nadie (not alguien) |
| I never eat meat. (→ *No como carne nunca — hesitant) | **No como carne nunca.** / **Nunca como carne.** | Both are correct — lean into the double negative |
| I don't want to go either. (→ *No quiero ir también.) | **No quiero ir tampoco.** | también → tampoco in negative contexts |

**Preemption script:** "English teaches you that double negatives are wrong. In Spanish, they are required. 'No tengo nada' literally translates as 'I don't have nothing' — and that is the only correct way to say it. 'No tengo algo' is the error. Your English brain will resist this. Let it be uncomfortable — that discomfort means you are learning something genuinely new, not just translating."

## Common Errors

1. **Avoiding double negatives:** *"No tengo algo"* instead of "No tengo **nada**." — L1 interference. The most common and persistent error. English speakers feel that nada after no is "wrong" and substitute algo. Correct firmly and consistently — this pattern must override the English instinct.
2. **Cuál vs qué confusion:** *"¿Cuál libro lees?"* instead of "¿**Qué** libro lees?" or *"¿Qué es tu nombre?"* instead of "¿**Cuál** es tu nombre?" — Developmental. The distinction is subtle: qué before nouns and for definitions, cuál for selection and specific identification. Teach through examples, not abstract rules.
3. **Forgetting accent marks on question words:** Writing *"que"* instead of "**qué**," *"donde"* instead of "**dónde**." — Developmental. These accents are not optional — they distinguish question words from relative pronouns (que = that, qué = what). Reinforce in writing practice.
4. **"Algo" instead of "nada" after "no":** *"No tengo algo que hacer"* instead of "No tengo **nada** que hacer." — L1 interference. A variant of error #1, appearing in more complex sentences. The rule is absolute: if no precedes the verb, all indefinites after it must be negative (nada, nadie, nunca, ninguno, tampoco).
5. **Adding "do" to questions:** *"¿Haces tú hablar español?"* (attempting "Do you speak Spanish?"). — L1 interference. English "do/does" has no Spanish equivalent. The verb alone forms the question. Usually resolves quickly but can surface under cognitive load.

## Teaching Sequence

### Stage 1: Noticing
Present 6 question-answer pairs. Do not explain the question words first.

- ¿**Dónde** vives? — Vivo en la ciudad.
- ¿**Qué** comes? — Como una ensalada.
- ¿**Quién** es ella? — Es mi hermana.
- ¿**Cuándo** estudias? — Estudio por la noche.
- ¿**Cómo** llegas al trabajo? — Camino.
- ¿**Por qué** estudias español? — Porque me gusta.

Ask: "Look at the question words. Can you guess what each one means based on the answer?" Then present 4 negative sentences and ask what pattern they see:

- No tengo nada.
- No conozco a nadie.
- Nunca como carne.
- No estudio tampoco.

Ask: "How many negative words do you see in each sentence? How does this compare to English?"

### Stage 2: Controlled Practice
Two drill types. Drill 1: Transform statements into questions using the appropriate question word. "Vivo en México" → "¿Dónde vives?" / "Estudio español" → "¿Qué estudias?" Drill 2: Negate sentences using the correct negative pair. "Tengo algo" → "No tengo nada." / "Conozco a alguien aquí" → "No conozco a nadie aquí." / "Siempre como carne" → "No como carne nunca." or "Nunca como carne."

### Stage 3: Guided Production
Prompt: "Ask me 10 questions about my life — where I live, what I do, who I know, when I work, why I study Spanish." The learner must select the correct question word for each question and form it naturally. After the tutor answers, prompt follow-up questions that require negation: "Now ask me what I do NOT do, or what I never do."

Scaffolding: Provide the question word bank (qué, quién, dónde, cuándo, cómo, por qué, cuánto, cuál) for reference. Remove after 5-6 correct questions.

### Stage 4: Communicative Practice
Prompt: "We are playing an interview game. You are interviewing me for a magazine article. Find out everything about my life — my work, my family, my hobbies, my habits." This requires sustained question production with varied question words, follow-up questions, and natural negation in responses. Focus is entirely on meaning and communication flow.

Alternative: "You and I are planning a trip together. Ask me questions to find out my preferences — and tell me what you do NOT like, what you never do on vacation, and what you do not want."

## Dialect Notes
Question formation and negation are consistent across all Spanish dialects. The double negative is standard and required everywhere — it is not colloquial or regional. Minor variation exists in informal speech patterns: some speakers drop the initial ¿ in casual texting or social media, but in all formal and educational contexts, the inverted question mark is required. The cuál/qué distinction is standard across all dialects. See `curriculum/dialect-notes.yaml` for any regional question-formation preferences.

## Signs of Acquisition
- **Scaffolded:** Correctly selects question words in structured drills. Produces double negatives when prompted. Can negate any affirmative sentence using the correct negative pair (algo→nada, alguien→nadie, siempre→nunca).
- **Unscaffolded:** Forms questions with correct question words in free conversation without pausing to recall. Uses double negatives naturally — "No tengo nada" flows without hesitation or self-correction.
- **Integrated:** Sustains question-and-answer exchanges across multiple turns. Negation patterns are automatic even in complex sentences. The cuál/qué distinction is applied correctly in most contexts. Double negatives no longer feel "wrong" — the learner has overridden the English instinct.

## Connection Points
Questions are the engine of all conversation practice — without them, the learner cannot direct a conversation or request information. Every subsequent session uses questions as the primary interaction tool. Negation patterns recur in all phases and become more complex: no...nada expands to "no hay nadie que..." (subjunctive trigger in Phase C). The cuál/qué distinction becomes increasingly important as questions grow in complexity. Double negative mastery here prevents fossilization of the English pattern, which becomes much harder to correct later. Common regression trigger: under cognitive load (new grammar concept, complex topic), learners revert to avoiding double negatives. Brief negation drills as warm-ups prevent this regression.
