# Relative Clauses — Connecting Ideas

## Overview
Relative clauses let the learner combine two short sentences into one complex one: "I met a woman. She speaks three languages." becomes "I met a woman **who** speaks three languages." Spanish uses several relative pronouns -- **que**, **quien**, **donde**, **lo que**, and others -- each with its own rules. Mastering relative clauses transforms choppy speech into fluid, connected discourse.

## When to Teach
- Phase: C
- Prerequisites: C-01-present-subjunctive
- L1 interference to preempt: none listed in l1-interference.yaml

## The Pattern

### The Core Relative Pronouns

| Pronoun | Use | English Equivalent |
|---------|-----|--------------------|
| **que** | Universal -- people and things, most common relative pronoun | that, which, who |
| **quien / quienes** | People only, typically after a preposition | who, whom |
| **donde** | Places | where |
| **lo que** | Abstract ideas, entire clauses, "the thing that" | what, that which |
| **el/la/los/las que** | Specific referent from a group, "the one(s) that" | the one that, those who |
| **cuyo/a/os/as** | Possession -- agrees with the noun it modifies, not the owner | whose |

### Que: The Workhorse

**Que** is the most common relative pronoun by far. It works for people and things, subjects and objects:

- **Subject:** La mujer **que** habla es mi profesora. (The woman who is speaking is my teacher.)
- **Object:** El libro **que** leí es excelente. (The book that I read is excellent.)

Unlike English, **que** is never omitted. In English you can say "The book I read" -- in Spanish, "El libro que leí" always requires **que**.

### Quien / Quienes: People After Prepositions

**Quien** (singular) and **quienes** (plural) refer only to people. Their primary role is after prepositions:

- La persona **con quien** hablé es muy amable. (The person with whom I spoke is very kind.)
- Los amigos **a quienes** invité no vinieron. (The friends whom I invited didn't come.)
- El profesor **para quien** trabajé era estricto. (The professor for whom I worked was strict.)

Without a preposition, **que** is preferred over **quien** for people:
- La persona **que** vi... (preferred) vs La persona **a quien** vi... (formal but less common)

### Donde: Places

**Donde** replaces location references:

- La ciudad **donde** nací es pequeña. (The city where I was born is small.)
- El restaurante **donde** comimos era barato. (The restaurant where we ate was cheap.)

With prepositions, **donde** combines naturally:
- La casa **de donde** vengo... (The house from where I come...)
- El lugar **hacia donde** vamos... (The place toward where we are going...)

### Lo Que: The Abstract Relative

**Lo que** refers to ideas, situations, or entire clauses -- not specific nouns:

- **Lo que** me dijiste es verdad. (What you told me is true.)
- No entiendo **lo que** pasó. (I don't understand what happened.)
- **Lo que** más me gusta es la música. (What I like most is the music.)

**Lo que** is NOT the same as **que**:
- "El libro **que** leí" = The book that I read (specific noun).
- "**Lo que** leí" = What I read (abstract, no specific noun).

### El/La/Los/Las Que: The Specific One

These identify a particular member of a group:

- De todos los libros, **el que** más me gustó fue este. (Of all the books, the one I liked most was this one.)
- **Las que** llegaron primero ganaron un premio. (The ones who arrived first won a prize.)

### Cuyo/a/os/as: Possession

**Cuyo** means "whose" and agrees in gender and number with the **possessed noun** (not the owner):

- El autor **cuyo** libro leí... (The author whose book I read...) -- cuyo agrees with "libro" (masculine singular)
- La mujer **cuya** hija estudia aquí... (The woman whose daughter studies here...) -- cuya agrees with "hija" (feminine singular)
- Los países **cuyos** líderes firmaron... (The countries whose leaders signed...) -- cuyos agrees with "líderes" (masculine plural)

**Cuyo** is relatively formal. In casual speech, speakers often restructure: "El autor -- leí su libro -- ..." In written and formal Spanish, cuyo is expected.

### Subjunctive in Relative Clauses

This is the key connection to C-01. Use **subjunctive** in the relative clause when the antecedent is **uncertain, nonexistent, or hypothetical**:

| Context | Mood | Example |
|---------|------|---------|
| Known/existing | Indicative | Tengo un amigo **que habla** japonés. (I have a friend who speaks Japanese.) |
| Unknown/sought | Subjunctive | Busco un amigo **que hable** japonés. (I'm looking for a friend who speaks Japanese.) |
| Nonexistent | Subjunctive | No hay nadie **que sepa** la respuesta. (There is nobody who knows the answer.) |
| Existing | Indicative | Conozco a alguien **que sabe** la respuesta. (I know someone who knows the answer.) |

The distinction: **indicative** = the thing exists and I know it. **Subjunctive** = I haven't found it, it may not exist, or I'm describing what I want rather than what I have.

## Examples in Context

1. La película **que** vimos anoche fue increíble. (The movie that we saw last night was incredible.)
2. El amigo **con quien** viajé habla cuatro idiomas. (The friend with whom I traveled speaks four languages.)
3. La ciudad **donde** crecí ha cambiado mucho. (The city where I grew up has changed a lot.)
4. **Lo que** necesitas es descansar. (What you need is to rest.)
5. De todas las opciones, **la que** prefiero es la primera. (Of all the options, the one I prefer is the first.)
6. El profesor **cuyas** clases tomé se jubiló. (The professor whose classes I took retired.)
7. Busco un restaurante **que sirva** comida mexicana. (I'm looking for a restaurant that serves Mexican food.) -- subjunctive
8. Tengo un restaurante **que sirve** comida mexicana excelente. (I have a restaurant that serves excellent Mexican food.) -- indicative
9. No conozco a nadie **que haya viajado** a Japón. (I don't know anyone who has traveled to Japan.) -- subjunctive
10. **Lo que** me sorprendió fue su amabilidad. (What surprised me was his kindness.)

## L1 Interference

**The core problems:** English allows omitting relative pronouns ("The book I read") -- Spanish never does. English uses "who/whom/that/which" somewhat interchangeably -- Spanish has stricter rules about when to use que vs quien. English "whose" does not change form -- Spanish cuyo agrees with the possessed noun.

**Common L1-driven mistakes:**

| English Instinct | Wrong | Correct | Why |
|-----------------|-------|---------|-----|
| Omit the relative pronoun | El libro leí... | El libro **que** leí... | Que is never omitted |
| Use "que" after a preposition for people | La persona con que hablé | La persona con **quien** hablé | Use quien after prepositions for people |
| Keep "whose" invariable | El autor cuyo hija... | El autor **cuya** hija... | Cuyo agrees with the possessed noun |
| Use indicative for hypothetical | Busco alguien que sabe... | Busco alguien que **sepa**... | Uncertain antecedent requires subjunctive |

**Preemption script:** "Relative clauses in Spanish work a lot like English, with one big difference: you can never drop the relative pronoun. In English you say 'the book I read' -- in Spanish it's always 'el libro que leí.' Also, when you're looking for something that might not exist, the verb in the relative clause switches to subjunctive."

## Common Errors

1. **Omitting que.** "El libro leí" instead of "El libro que leí." English freely drops "that" in object relative clauses. Spanish never does. -- L1 interference. Drill by having the learner combine two sentences into one, always requiring a pronoun.
2. **Using que instead of quien after prepositions.** "La persona con que hablé" instead of "con quien hablé." While "con la que" is an alternative, "con que" alone is not standard. -- Developmental. Practice preposition + quien combinations explicitly.
3. **Wrong agreement on cuyo.** "El hombre cuyo esposa..." instead of "cuya esposa." Cuyo agrees with what is possessed, not the possessor. -- L1 interference (English "whose" is invariable). Provide explicit agreement drills.
4. **Using indicative after uncertain antecedents.** "Busco un libro que es interesante" instead of "que sea interesante." The indicative/subjunctive split in relative clauses is subtle and takes time to internalize. -- Developmental / L1 interference. Contrast "tengo un libro que es..." vs "busco un libro que sea..."
5. **Confusing lo que and que.** "Lo que libro leí" or using "que" for abstract ideas. Lo que has no specific antecedent; que does. -- Developmental. Drill the distinction: "El libro que..." (specific noun) vs "Lo que pasó..." (abstract/no noun).

## Teaching Sequence

### Stage 1: Noticing
Present pairs of simple sentences, then the combined version. Ask the learner to identify the "connecting word" and what it replaces:

- "Tengo un amigo. Mi amigo habla japonés." → "Tengo un amigo **que** habla japonés."
- "Fui a un restaurante. Comí en el restaurante." → "Fui al restaurante **donde** comí."
- "Hablé con una mujer. La mujer es doctora." → "La mujer **con quien** hablé es doctora."
- "No entendí algo. Algo pasó." → "No entendí **lo que** pasó."

After reviewing, ask: "When do you see que? When donde? When quien? When lo que?"

### Stage 2: Controlled Practice
**Sentence combination drill:** Give two sentences, learner produces one using the appropriate relative pronoun:

1. "Vi una película. La película fue aburrida." → "La película que vi fue aburrida."
2. "Viajé con un amigo. Mi amigo es de Colombia." → "El amigo con quien viajé es de Colombia."
3. "Nací en una ciudad. La ciudad es grande." → "La ciudad donde nací es grande."
4. "Me dijiste algo. Algo me sorprendió." → "Lo que me dijiste me sorprendió."
5. "Conozco a una mujer. Su hija estudia medicina." → "Conozco a una mujer cuya hija estudia medicina."

**Subjunctive contrast drill:** Fill in with indicative or subjunctive:
- "Tengo un coche que (ser) _____ rojo." → "es" (exists)
- "Quiero un coche que (ser) _____ rojo." → "sea" (hypothetical)

### Stage 3: Guided Production
Prompt: "Describe your ideal home, job, and vacation. Use 'Busco una casa que...' 'Quiero un trabajo que...' 'Necesito unas vacaciones donde...' -- remember, since these are things you're looking for, the relative clause uses subjunctive."

Then switch: "Now tell me about your actual home, job, and daily routine. Use 'Tengo una casa que...' 'Mi trabajo es algo que...' -- these are real, so indicative."

The contrast between hypothetical and real naturally drills the subjunctive/indicative split in relative clauses.

### Stage 4: Communicative Practice
Prompt: "Tell me about the most interesting people in your life. Who is the person who taught you the most? The one whose advice you value? What is the thing that surprised you most this year? Is there something you're looking for that you haven't found yet?"

This naturally elicits que (people/things), quien (after prepositions), cuyo (possession), lo que (abstract), and the subjunctive contrast (looking for something). Let the learner speak freely. Note relative pronoun errors for post-conversation review.

## Dialect Notes
Relative pronoun usage is largely consistent across dialects. Minor regional notes:

- In **informal speech across Latin America**, cuyo is rare. Speakers restructure: "El hombre -- su esposa trabaja aquí -- ..." rather than "El hombre cuya esposa trabaja aquí." Teach cuyo for recognition and written Spanish, but don't expect it in casual conversation.
- **Queísmo** (using "que" where a preposition + que is expected) occurs in some dialects: "El tema que hablamos" instead of "El tema del que hablamos." This is considered non-standard. Teach the prepositional form.
- In **Mexican Spanish**, relative clause structures follow standard patterns without significant regional variation.

## Signs of Acquisition
- **Scaffolded:** Correctly combines sentences using que, quien, donde, and lo que. Produces cuyo with correct agreement. Chooses subjunctive vs indicative in relative clauses based on whether the antecedent is known or hypothetical.
- **Unscaffolded:** Spontaneously uses relative clauses to connect ideas instead of speaking in short, choppy sentences. Never omits the relative pronoun. Uses lo que for abstract references naturally.
- **Integrated:** Maintains correct relative pronoun choice while managing other grammatical complexity (tense, subjunctive triggers, compound tenses). Uses the subjunctive/indicative contrast in relative clauses without prompting. Cuyo appears occasionally in more formal or narrative speech.

## Connection Points
Relative clauses integrate heavily with the subjunctive system and narrative discourse:

- **C-01** (present subjunctive) is the prerequisite. The subjunctive/indicative contrast in relative clauses is one of the most natural applications of subjunctive usage.
- **C-02** (subjunctive triggers) adds another context for subjunctive: the relative clause with uncertain antecedent is a trigger category not covered by the WEIRDO framework.
- **C-08** (indirect speech) uses relative structures in reported questions: "Me preguntó lo que pasó."
- **D-04** (passive voice and se constructions) uses relative clauses extensively: "La casa que se vendió..." (The house that was sold...).
- **D-06** (nuanced connectors) allows building longer, more complex sentences that incorporate relative clauses alongside connectors.

Common regression trigger: when C-08 (indirect speech) introduces "me dijo que..." the learner may briefly confuse the conjunction "que" (that he said) with the relative pronoun "que" (that/which). Clarify that "dijo que" is a reporting structure, while "el libro que" is a relative clause. Context makes the distinction clear.
