# Register Shifting — Social Judgment and Contextual Awareness

## Overview
The grammar of register shifting (tú/usted/vos forms, vocabulary changes, verb mood shifts) is taught in D-05. This cultural guide addresses the harder half: **knowing when to shift**. Native speakers develop this instinct from childhood through thousands of social interactions. For an English speaker, whose language uses one "you" for everyone, this social antenna must be consciously built. Getting the forms right but the timing wrong — calling your friend's grandmother "tú" or your close colleague "usted" — creates awkwardness that no amount of grammatical accuracy can fix. This guide develops the learner's ability to read social situations and make real-time register decisions.

## When to Teach
- Phase: D (cultural awareness component)
- Parallel to: D-05-register-shifting (grammar forms)
- Prerequisites: Learner can already produce both tú and usted forms accurately. This guide assumes the mechanics are in place and focuses entirely on social deployment.
- Enablers: Experience with Spanish speakers (even through media) helps contextualize the social rules.

## Key Patterns

### The Decision Framework

When meeting someone new or entering a conversation, native speakers unconsciously evaluate four factors:

| Factor | Tú signal | Usted signal |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| **Age relative to you** | Same age or younger | Noticeably older |
| **Social hierarchy** | Peer, subordinate, or no hierarchy | Boss, teacher, doctor, official |
| **Setting** | Casual (bar, park, party) | Formal (office, courtroom, ceremony) |
| **Relationship warmth** | Known, close, or signaled informality | Unknown, distant, or signaled formality |

**The default rule for the learner:** When in doubt, start with usted. It is almost never offensive to be too formal — but it can be offensive to be too informal. Usted is the safe starting point. Wait for the other person to offer tú.

### The "Tú Offer" — Recognizing the Shift Signal

One of the most important social moments in Spanish is when someone signals that tú is appropriate. This can be:

- **Explicit:** "Puedes tutearme" / "Trátame de tú" (You can use tú with me)
- **Implicit:** They switch to tú verb forms when addressing you
- **Environmental:** Everyone at the gathering is using tú; joining the group implies tú
- **Generational:** A younger person meeting another younger person — tú is assumed

**The learner's job:** Recognize the signal and reciprocate. Continuing with usted after someone offers tú signals coldness or excessive distance.

### Situations That Confuse English Speakers

| Situation | English instinct | Spanish reality |
|-----------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Waiter at a restaurant | Casual equals, first-name basis | Usted — it is a service interaction, not a friendship |
| Colleague you see daily | First-name = informal | Depends on workplace culture. Many offices use tú among peers, usted with superiors |
| Friend's parent | First-name if offered | Almost always usted, even if they use your first name |
| Uber driver | Casual chat | Usted — stranger in a transactional context |
| Child (not yours) | Informal | Tú — children always receive tú |
| Elderly stranger asking directions | Helpful and casual | Usted — age demands respect regardless of setting |
| A peer at a party | Normal socializing | Tú — social setting with peers signals informality |
| Your partner's family (first meeting) | Depends on their warmth | Usted until explicitly told otherwise, even if they are warm |

### Dynamic Shifting Within a Conversation

Register is not fixed for a relationship — it can evolve within a single interaction or over weeks:

- **First meeting → friendship:** Start with usted, shift to tú as comfort grows (often happens within one long conversation if rapport develops)
- **Professional → social:** Colleagues who use usted at work may use tú at after-work drinks
- **Anger or confrontation:** Some speakers downshift to tú to signal aggression or condescension ("¿Tú quién te crees?"). Others upshift to usted to signal cold distance ("Usted no me habla así")
- **Humor:** Friends may use exaggerated usted for comic effect ("¿Me haría usted el favor de pasarme la sal?" at a casual dinner = funny)

### The Group Register Problem

When speaking to a group with mixed register expectations (e.g., your friend and their boss at the same table), the learner must:
1. Address each person in their appropriate register when speaking directly to them
2. Use usted for the group when addressing everyone ("¿Ustedes quieren algo de tomar?")
3. Avoid accidentally using tú forms when glancing at the formal person

This is a high-cognitive-load task. Practice it explicitly.

## L1 Interference

**The core problem:** English speakers have zero muscle memory for register decisions. Every "you" in English is the same word. The learner must build a habit of asking "who am I talking to?" before every utterance — a question that English never forces them to ask.

**Specific interference patterns:**

1. **The friendliness trap.** American English culture often equates friendliness with informality. A warm smile and first-name basis in English means "we're comfortable." In Spanish, warmth and formality coexist. A waiter who smiles and chats warmly still expects usted. The learner interprets social warmth as a tú signal when it isn't.

2. **The equality assumption.** English speakers default to treating everyone as equals in language (even if not in behavior). The idea of linguistically marking hierarchy feels uncomfortable or even wrong. The learner may resist using usted because it "feels like I'm creating distance." Reframe: usted is not distance — it is respect. Using tú with a stranger is not equality — it is presumption.

3. **The "everyone does it" bias.** After hearing a few Spaniards use tú liberally, the learner generalizes: "Spanish speakers use tú with everyone." This is regionally true (Spain) but dangerously wrong for Mexico, Colombia, and most of Latin America. Reinforce that the target dialect's norms are what matter.

4. **Overcorrection paralysis.** After being corrected for using tú inappropriately, some learners freeze and use usted with everyone, including close friends. This creates a different social problem — sounding distant and robotic. The goal is fluid shifting, not permanent formality.

## Teaching Sequence

### Stage 1: Noticing
Present 3-4 short video clips or written dialogues showing natural register shifts:
- A customer ordering at a café (usted with waiter)
- Two friends meeting for coffee (tú)
- A job interview (usted)
- A party where two strangers become friends over the evening (usted → tú transition)

Ask the learner to identify: "What register is each person using? When does it change? Why do you think it changes?" Focus entirely on observation — no production yet.

### Stage 2: Awareness
Present the four-factor decision framework (age, hierarchy, setting, warmth). Give 10-12 social scenarios and have the learner predict the register:
- "You're meeting your Spanish tutor's mother at a dinner party."
- "You're playing pickup soccer and need to ask a teammate to pass."
- "You're emailing a company about a billing error."
- "You're texting a Mexican friend you've known for years."

Discuss the answers. Where the learner gets it wrong, explore the reasoning. Map back to the four factors.

### Stage 3: Guided Practice
Role-play with explicit register requirements:
1. **The café scenario:** Order from a waiter (usted), then your friend arrives (tú), then your friend's professor joins (usted). Practice switching cleanly.
2. **The "tú offer":** The tutor plays a stranger who, midway through the conversation, says "Puedes tutearme." The learner must recognize the signal and shift.
3. **The overcorrection check:** The tutor plays a close friend. If the learner uses usted, the tutor responds with playful confusion: "¿Por qué me hablas de usted? ¿Estás enojado conmigo?"

Recast when the learner uses the wrong register. Provide the four-factor reasoning when correcting.

### Stage 4: Natural Production
Embed in free conversation. Stop correcting register explicitly — instead, react naturally:
- If the learner uses tú inappropriately in a role-play, the "character" looks slightly taken aback.
- If the learner uses usted with a "friend" character, the friend acts confused or distant.

The learner must read the social reaction and self-correct. This builds the instinct rather than relying on explicit rules.

## Signs of Acquisition
- **Scaffolded:** Correctly identifies the appropriate register for described social situations. Can explain why using the four-factor framework. Shifts register when explicitly told to.
- **Unscaffolded:** Shifts registers appropriately without prompting in role-play scenarios. Recognizes the "tú offer" and reciprocates. Maintains register consistency within an exchange.
- **Integrated:** Makes real-time register decisions in unstructured conversation. Handles group situations with mixed register requirements. Recovers from register errors naturally (self-corrects mid-sentence). Uses register shifts for social effect (humor, emphasis) rather than just compliance.

## Examples in Context

1. **First meeting:** You meet your Mexican host family. Address the parents with usted, the teenage children with tú. If the mother says "Puedes tutearme," shift — but keep usted with the father until he offers the same.
2. **At work:** Your Mexican colleague greets you with "¿Qué onda?" (very informal). Reciprocate with tú. When their boss walks over, both of you shift to usted without discussion.
3. **Making a friend:** At a language exchange, you start with usted. After 20 minutes of good conversation, the other person says "No me hables de usted, me haces sentir viejo." Laugh, shift to tú, and stay there.
4. **The awkward moment:** You accidentally use tú with an elderly woman at church. She doesn't correct you, but her expression tightens slightly. Notice the social feedback and shift to usted for the rest of the interaction.
5. **Humor:** At a dinner with close friends, someone asks you to pass the salt. You respond with exaggerated formality: "Con mucho gusto, le paso la sal inmediatamente." Everyone laughs — you're using register mismatch as humor, which means you understand the norms well enough to break them.

## Dialect Notes
Register expectations are one of the most regionally variable aspects of Spanish. The target dialect (Mexican Spanish) is moderately formal — more usted-expected situations than Spain, fewer than Colombia. See `curriculum/dialect-notes.yaml` for regional specifics. Key differences:

- **Mexico:** Tú/usted system. Usted expected with strangers, elders, service workers. Some families use usted between parents and children.
- **Spain:** Heavy tú culture. Strangers of similar age often use tú immediately. Usted reserved for clear age/authority gaps.
- **Colombia:** Very formal. Usted used even between close friends in Bogotá and highland regions.
- **Argentina:** Vos replaces tú. Very informal culture overall. Usted rare except in very formal contexts.

If the learner consumes media from multiple regions, expect confusion. Anchor to the target dialect's norms and explicitly note when other dialects differ.
