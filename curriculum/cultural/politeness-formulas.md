# Politeness Formulas

## Overview
Spanish politeness operates on a different axis than English. English speakers tend toward brevity and directness ("Can I have...?"), while Spanish speakers employ elaborate softening strategies that signal respect, social awareness, and warmth. A learner who translates English politeness directly will sound blunt, cold, or even rude — not because the words are wrong, but because the pragmatic packaging is missing. Mastering these formulas is what separates "grammatically correct" from "socially competent."

## When to Teach
- Phase: B (introduce after basic verb conjugation and conditional mood exposure)
- Prerequisites: A-01 present tense, C-04 conditional (for podría forms — early exposure here; formally acquired in Phase C), basic question formation
- Enablers: Learner has enough vocabulary to make real requests in conversation practice

## Key Patterns

### Softening Requests — The Directness Scale

Spanish has a gradient of directness for making requests. Native speakers default to the middle range; learners default to the extremes.

| Level | Spanish | English equivalent | Social signal |
|-------|---------|-------------------|---------------|
| Very direct | Dame el agua. | Give me the water. | Rude with strangers, fine with close family |
| Direct | Quiero un café. | I want a coffee. | Acceptable in cafes, abrupt in formal settings |
| Standard polite | ¿Me da un café? | Could you get me a coffee? | Normal in restaurants and shops (Spain: ¿Me pone…?) |
| Soft polite | ¿Podría traerme un café? | Could you possibly bring me a coffee? | Professional settings, strangers |
| Very soft | ¿Sería tan amable de traerme un café? | Would you be so kind as to bring me a coffee? | Formal letters, elderly speakers |

Key phrases to drill:
- **¿Me puede/podría + infinitive?** — the workhorse polite request
- **¿Sería posible + infinitive?** — formal contexts
- **Disculpe, ¿le importaría + infinitive?** — asking a stranger for a favor

### Disagreeing Politely

English speakers say "No, I think..." or "I disagree." Spanish speakers almost never disagree head-on.

| English instinct | Spanish equivalent | Why it works |
|-----------------|-------------------|--------------|
| No, that's wrong. | Bueno, no estoy tan seguro... | "Well, I'm not so sure..." — softens the contradiction |
| I disagree. | A mí me parece que... | "It seems to me that..." — frames as personal perspective |
| That's not true. | Sí, pero ¿no crees que...? | "Yes, but don't you think...?" — validates before redirecting |
| You're wrong about that. | Entiendo lo que dices, pero... | "I understand what you're saying, but..." — acknowledges first |

### Apologizing — Beyond "Lo Siento"

| Situation | Spanish | Notes |
|-----------|---------|-------|
| Bumping into someone | Perdón / Perdona | Quick, reflexive — like "sorry" |
| Interrupting | Disculpa / Disculpe (formal) | More formal than perdón |
| Serious apology | Lo siento mucho, de verdad | "I'm truly sorry" — reserve for genuine regret |
| Taking blame | Ha sido culpa mía | "It was my fault" — direct ownership |
| Asking forgiveness | Te pido perdón / disculpas | Stronger than lo siento |

### Gratitude Beyond "Gracias"

| Level | Spanish | When to use |
|-------|---------|-------------|
| Quick thanks | Gracias | Everyday transactions |
| Warm thanks | Muchas gracias, muy amable | Someone went slightly out of their way |
| Sincere thanks | Te lo agradezco mucho | A real favor |
| Formal/deep | Le estoy muy agradecido/a | Professional or significant help |
| Deflecting thanks | No hay de qué / De nada / No es nada | When someone thanks you |

## L1 Interference

**English habit:** Politeness is achieved mainly through "please," "thank you," and indirect question forms ("Could you...?"). The words themselves carry the politeness.

**Spanish reality:** Politeness lives in verb mood (conditional vs. present), diminutives (un momentito vs. un momento), discourse markers (mira, fíjate, es que...), and elaborate preambles. Saying "por favor" alone is not enough to sound polite — the surrounding construction matters more.

**Common errors:**
- Using "quiero" (I want) for requests in formal settings — sounds demanding
- Saying "no" flatly when disagreeing — sounds confrontational
- Using "lo siento" for minor bumps when "perdón" is more natural
- Skipping the preamble: launching into a request without "disculpe" or "perdona"
- Translating "please" as "por favor" tacked onto a blunt command — "Dame eso, por favor" is less polite than "¿Me lo podrías dar?"

## Teaching Sequence

### Stage 1: Noticing
Play or present 3-4 short dialogues: one at a coffee shop, one asking a stranger for directions, one disagreeing in conversation. Ask: "How does the speaker ask for what they want? Notice they never say 'quiero' — what do they say instead?" Have the learner identify the softening strategies used.

### Stage 2: Awareness
Present the directness scale. Give the learner the same request scenario (asking a waiter, asking a boss, asking a friend) and discuss which level fits each. Practice transforming "quiero X" into "¿podría X?" and "¿me da X?" (Spain: "¿me pone X?") Compare disagreement styles: give an opinion and have the learner practice disagreeing using the Spanish formulas rather than direct negation.

### Stage 3: Guided Practice
Role-play scenarios with escalating formality:
- Ordering food at a casual restaurant
- Asking a coworker for help with something
- Calling a hotel to change a reservation
- Politely declining an invitation

Provide the formulas on a reference card. Recast when the learner reverts to overly direct forms.

### Stage 4: Natural Production
Embed in free conversation. When the learner makes a request, expresses disagreement, or apologizes, note whether they reach for the appropriate politeness level naturally. Remove the reference card. Introduce scenarios where the learner must shift register mid-conversation (e.g., talking to a friend when their boss walks in).

## Signs of Acquisition
- **Scaffolded:** Can produce the correct politeness formula when given a situation and formality level. Uses "podría" for polite requests in structured activities.
- **Unscaffolded:** Naturally softens requests in free conversation without prompting. Disagrees using hedging language rather than flat "no." Matches formality to context without being told the register.
- **Integrated:** Shifts smoothly between casual and formal registers in the same conversation. Uses preambles and discourse markers (es que, mira, fíjate) to frame requests and disagreements naturally.

## Examples in Context

1. **Café:** ¿Me da un café, por favor? (Could you get me a coffee, please? — standard cafe request; Spain: ¿Me pone un cortado?)
2. **Stranger:** Disculpe, ¿podría decirme dónde está la estación? (Excuse me, could you tell me where the station is?)
3. **Declining:** Me encantaría, pero es que tengo un compromiso. (I'd love to, but the thing is I have a commitment.)
4. **Disagreeing:** Sí, tienes razón en eso, pero a mí me parece que... (Yes, you're right about that, but it seems to me that...)
5. **Apologizing:** Perdona que te moleste, pero ¿podrías bajar la música? (Sorry to bother you, but could you turn down the music?)
6. **Thanking:** Te lo agradezco un montón, de verdad no tenías por qué hacerlo. (I really appreciate it, you truly didn't have to do that.)

## Dialect Notes
Mexican Spanish tends toward higher politeness levels in daily interactions (more "disculpe," more conditional forms). Caribbean Spanish may be more direct and warm without it reading as rude. Castilian Spanish uses "perdona" (tú) more freely than Latin American "disculpe" (usted) in casual settings. See `curriculum/dialect-notes.yaml` for regional specifics.
