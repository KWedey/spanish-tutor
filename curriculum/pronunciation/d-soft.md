# The Soft D Between Vowels

## Phase
Phase B — introduced once learners have enough vocabulary to encounter intervocalic D frequently (nada, todo, cansado, ciudad).

## The Sound

Spanish D has two realizations depending on position, just like B/V (see `b-v-equivalence.md`):

| Position | IPA | Articulation | Examples |
|----------|-----|-------------|---------|
| After a pause, after n, or after l | /d/ | Tongue tip contacts upper teeth — like English D | donde, anda, el día |
| Between vowels, after most consonants | /ð/ | Tongue tip approaches upper teeth but does NOT make full contact | nada, todo, cansado |

The /ð/ is the same sound as the English "th" in "this," "the," "that," "there." Your tongue approaches the upper teeth but air flows around it continuously. There is no stop, no release — just friction.

So "nada" sounds like "na-tha," "todo" sounds like "to-tho," and "cansado" sounds like "cansa-tho."

## Common English Speaker Errors

**Always using the hard D.** English has only one D phoneme — a full tongue-to-ridge stop /d/. English speakers apply this everywhere in Spanish, even between vowels. The result is grammatically correct but sounds mechanical and noticeably non-native. "Nada" said with a hard D in the middle is understood, but marks the speaker as foreign.

**Using English "th" in the wrong position.** The soft /ð/ only applies between vowels and after most consonants. Word-initial D and D after n or l remain hard. "Donde" starts with /d/, not /ð/.

**Dropping the D entirely.** In very casual native speech, the final D in words like "verdad," "ciudad," and "usted" can weaken to near-zero or disappear. Learners sometimes adopt this without knowing where it applies. At Phase B, aim to always produce the /ð/ — dropping is a later fluency refinement.

## Position Rule Summary

| Context | Pronunciation | Example |
|---------|--------------|---------|
| Start of utterance | /d/ (hard stop) | "Donde..." at start of sentence |
| After n | /d/ (hard stop) | anda, cuando, undo |
| After l | /d/ (hard stop) | el día, falda |
| Between vowels | /ð/ (soft, "th"-like) | nada, todo, cada, ciudad |
| After other consonants | /ð/ (soft) | verdad, cansado, pedido |

## Practice Words

| Word | Meaning | D position | Pronunciation |
|------|---------|-----------|--------------|
| nada | nothing | Between vowels | /ˈna.ða/ |
| todo | everything | Between vowels | /ˈto.ðo/ |
| cada | each | Between vowels | /ˈka.ða/ |
| cansado | tired | Between vowels (end) | /kan.ˈsa.ðo/ |
| ciudad | city | Between vowels (final) | /sjuˈðað/ |
| verdad | truth | Between vowels (final) | /beɾˈðað/ |
| dedo | finger | Mixed: initial /d/, then /ð/ | /ˈde.ðo/ |
| donde | where | Initial /d/ — stays hard | /ˈdon.de/ |
| anda | walk (command) | After n — stays hard | /ˈan.da/ |

Note "dedo": the first D is hard (word-initial), the second D is soft (between vowels). This single word demonstrates both rules.

## Practice Assignments

**Speechling recordings:**
- Record between-vowel soft D words: `nada`, `todo`, `cada`, `cansado`, `ciudad`, `verdad`, `pedido`, `cuidado`
- Record "dedo" alone — notice the switch from hard to soft D within one word
- Record a sentence: `Todo el mundo sabe que es verdad.`

**Forvo listening:**
- Listen to: `nada`, `todo`, `cansado`, `ciudad`, `verdad`, `cuidado`, `dedo`, `donde`
- Focus: Does the D between vowels sound like "th" in "the"? Compare initial D (donde) to intervocalic D (nada).

**Contrast drill:**
Say these pairs — the first has hard D (initial), the second has soft D (between vowels):
- `doy` → `todo` (hard then soft)
- `día` → `cada día` (hard then soft)
- `donde` → `nada` (both hard then soft)

**Self-narration target words:**
Flag intervocalic D words in daily narration: `todo`, `nada`, `cada`, `cansado`, `ciudad`, `pedido`, `verdad`.

## Signs of Acquisition
- Speechling coach reports consistent soft D between vowels in 3 or more sessions.
- Hard D is correctly maintained after n, l, and at word boundaries.
- "Dedo" is produced with the correct hard-then-soft alternation.
- Conversation practice shows no robotic hard-D-everywhere pattern in high-frequency words like "nada," "todo," "cansado."
